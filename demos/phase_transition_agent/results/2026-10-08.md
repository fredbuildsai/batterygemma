## 2. What Happens When Charged Above 4.2 V?

Charging a lithium-ion cell above its nominal voltage (e.g., above $4.2 \text{ V}$ for a standard 3.7 V cell) forces the cell to accept more energy, which leads to several potential, and often dangerous, outcomes:

### A. Increased Polarization and Overcharging (The Primary Concern)

When the cell is charged beyond its designed limit, the lithium intercalation process becomes less reversible and more irreversible.

* **Lithium Plating:** If the charging current is too high or the voltage is pushed too far, lithium ions can deposit directly onto the anode (or within the cathode structure) as metallic lithium ($\text{Li}^+$ plating). This is highly undesirable as it consumes active lithium inventory and can lead to dendritic growth.
* **SEI Layer Degradation:** The Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) layer on the anode is crucial for battery stability. Overcharging can destabilize this layer, leading to continuous electrolyte decomposition and further capacity fade.

### B. Cathode Degradation and Structural Changes (The Chemical Concern)

Charging NMC materials excessively can cause irreversible structural changes within the cathode material itself:

* **Transition Metal Oxidation:** At very high potentials, the transition metal ions ($\text{Ni}^{2+}$, $\text{Co}^{3+}$) within the NMC structure can be oxidized beyond their stable states. This can lead to the formation of higher-valence, less stable oxides or even the decomposition of the cathode structure.
* **Phase Change:** In extreme cases, the material structure might undergo irreversible phase transitions, leading to permanent capacity loss and internal stress buildup.

### C. Thermal Runaway Risk (The Safety Concern)

The most significant danger of overcharging is the increased potential for **thermal runaway**:

1. **Increased Internal Energy:** Storing excess charge (energy) within the cell increases the total internal energy.
2. **Exothermic Reactions:** If the cell is charged too fast or the internal resistance is high, localized, uncontrolled exothermic side reactions can occur.
3. **Decomposition:** These reactions generate heat faster than the cell can dissipate it. This heat accelerates further decomposition of the electrolyte and cathode material, leading to gas generation ($\text{CO}_2$, $\text{O}_2$) and rapid temperature rise, which can lead to venting, fire, or explosion.

---

## 3. Mitigation Strategies

To prevent these issues, battery management systems (BMS) are designed to monitor and control the charging process:

1. **Voltage Cutoff:** The BMS monitors the cell voltage and stops charging when it reaches a pre-set maximum voltage (e.g., $4.2 \text{ V}$ or $4.3 \text{ V}$ for Li-ion cells).
2. **Current Control:** The BMS regulates the charging current to ensure that the rate of energy input matches the rate of energy storage, preventing excessive heat generation.
3. **Temperature Monitoring:** If the cell temperature rises above a safe threshold (e.g., $55^\circ\text{C}$), the BMS will automatically halt charging and potentially initiate cooling protocols.

### Plain model

When nickel-rich NMC811 (LiNi₀.₈Mn₀.₁Co₀.₁O₂) cathodes are charged above 4.2 V vs. Li⁺/Li, several degradation mechanisms are triggered due to the high operating voltage pushing the material beyond its thermodynamic and structural stability window. NMC811 is particularly prone to these effects because of its high nickel content, which enhances capacity but reduces structural and surface stability.

Here’s what happens when NMC811 is charged above 4.2 V:

---

### **1. Oxygen Loss and Oxygen Evolution Reaction (OER)**
- At voltages >4.2 V, the oxidation of lattice oxygen (O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻) becomes thermodynamically favorable.
- This leads to **oxygen release** from the cathode surface, especially at grain boundaries and particle surfaces.
- Oxygen loss creates **oxygen vacancies**, which destabilize the layered structure and promote phase transitions.

### **2. Structural Degradation: Layered-to-Rock Salt Phase Transformation**
- Oxygen loss triggers cation migration: Ni²⁺ ions migrate from their octahedral sites in the transition metal layer into the lithium layer.
- This results in the formation of a **disordered rock-salt (DRS) phase** on the particle surface.
- The rock-salt phase is electrochemically inactive and increases impedance, reducing usable capacity and rate capability.

### **3. Transition Metal Dissolution**
- Especially Mn and Ni dissolve into the electrolyte due to acid attack (from HF generated by LiPF₆ hydrolysis) and direct chemical oxidation.
- Dissolved transition metals migrate to the anode, where they can:
  - Catalyze SEI growth (increasing impedance),
  - Plate as metallic deposits (causing internal shorts or lithium plating),
  - Degrade SEI stability.

### **4. Electrolyte Oxidation and CEI Formation**
- High voltage (>4.2 V) oxidizes common carbonate-based electrolytes (e.g., EC, DMC), leading to:
  - Formation of a **cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI)** layer.
  - Gas evolution (CO₂, CO, C₂H₄), causing cell swelling.
  - Consumption of lithium inventory and electrolyte, reducing cycle life and Coulombic efficiency.

### **5. Particle Cracking and Mechanical Degradation**
- Repeated lithiation/delithiation above 4.2 V induces anisotropic lattice strain (especially along the c-axis).
- In NMC811, the high Ni content exacerbates anisotropic volume changes → **microcracking** within secondary particles.
- Cracks expose fresh surfaces to electrolyte, accelerating side reactions and increasing impedance over time.

### **6. Voltage Fade and Capacity Fade**
- Progressive structural degradation (layered → spinel/rock-salt) leads to a gradual decrease in average operating voltage (**voltage fade**).
- Combined with loss of active lithium and active material, this results in **capacity fade**.
- NMC811 typically shows accelerated fade above 4.2 V; many commercial cells limit upper cutoff to 4.1–4.2 V to balance energy density and longevity.

### **7. Thermal Runaway Risk**
- Oxygen release and exothermic reactions between the charged cathode and electrolyte lower the onset temperature for thermal runaway.
- Charging above 4.2 V increases the amount of reactive oxygen and reduces thermal stability, posing safety risks — especially in large-format cells.

---

### **Mitigation Strategies (Industry Context)**
To enable higher voltage operation (>4.2 V) while preserving NMC811 performance:
- **Surface coatings** (Al₂O₃, ZrO₂, LiPO₄, etc.) to suppress oxygen loss and HF attack.
- **Doping** (Mg, Al, Ti, etc.) to stabilize the layered structure and suppress cation mixing.
- **Electrolyte additives** (e.g., LiPO₂F₂, TPP, LiDFOB) to form stable CEI and scavenge HF.
- **Single-crystal NMC811** to eliminate intergranular cracking.
- **Voltage-limited charging** (e.g., 4.1–4.2 V) remains common in EVs for longevity/safety trade-offs.

---

### **Practical Implication**
In most commercial lithium-ion batteries using NMC811, the **upper cutoff voltage is intentionally limited to 4.2 V or below** (often 4.15–4.20 V) to avoid rapid degradation. Charging consistently above 4.2 V significantly reduces cycle life (e.g., from 1000+ cycles to <500 cycles) and increases safety concerns — unless the cell is specifically engineered with advanced stabilizations.

> 💡 **Note**: Some next-generation cells (e.g., in high-energy silicon-anode or lithium-metal batteries) push NMC811 to 4.3–4.4 V with heavy stabilization, but this requires co-optimization of cathode, electrolyte, and anode — not just cathode alone.

If you're designing or testing NMC811-based cells, operating above 4.2 V without mitigation is generally **not recommended for long-term cycle life or safety**.

| Phenomenon | What happens above 4.2 V | Why it matters |
|------------|--------------------------|----------------|
| **Lattice‑oxygen release** | O²⁻ is oxidised to O⁰ (or O₂⁻) and leaves the crystal as O₂ gas. | Creates oxygen vacancies, destabilises the layered structure, and triggers side reactions with the electrolyte. |

| **Electrolyte oxidation** | The high cathode potential (> 4.3 V) exceeds the oxidative stability window of conventional carbonate electrolytes (≈ 4.2 V). | Generates HF, CO₂, and other acidic/oxidative species that attack the cathode surface and the SEI on the anode. |

## 1. The electrochemical window of NMC811

| Parameter | Typical value (vs. Li⁺/Li) |

The *theoretical* redox plateau for Ni²⁺/Ni⁴⁺ in NMC811 lies around 4.3 V. However, the practical upper limit is set by the stability of the cathode lattice and the electrolyte. ## 2. Structural changes in the bulk and at the surface

### 2.1 Bulk lattice contraction & oxygen vacancy formation
* **X‑ray diffraction (XRD) & neutron diffraction** studies (e.g., Li et al., *Nat. Energy* 2020) show a sudden decrease in the *c*‑axis lattice parameter when the cell is charged above ~4.25 V, consistent with loss of O²⁻ from the lattice.
* **Oxygen‑K edge X‑ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS)** reveals an increase in the O‑hole concentration (O⁻ character) that precedes O₂ evolution.

### 2.2 Surface reconstruction to rock‑salt/spinel
* **High‑resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR‑TEM)** images show a ~5–10 nm thick layer of NiO‑like rock‑salt phase forming on the particle surface after a single 4.35 V charge.
* **Depth‑profile XPS** detects a higher Ni²⁺/Ni³⁺ ratio and a loss of Li signal in the top few nanometres, confirming cation mixing and loss of the layered ordering.

### 2.3 Cation mixing (Ni²⁺ ↔ Li⁺)
* **Rietveld refinements** of XRD data indicate an increase in the Ni⁴⁺→Ni²⁺ reduction reaction, which is accompanied by Ni²⁺ migration into Li layers. This is thermodynamically favoured once oxygen vacancies appear.
